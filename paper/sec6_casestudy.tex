\section{Case Study: Citation Verification}
\label{sec:casestudy}

The six families of Section~\ref{sec:setup} are controlled by construction. To
check that the framework says something useful about a task people actually
deploy language models on, we apply it to reference generation---a setting
where fabricated output is a known and consequential failure mode, and where an
external registry supplies a near-perfect verifier for the price of an HTTP
request.

The task ``produce a real published reference on topic $X$'' maps onto the
framework exactly. A generator proposes $k$ candidate references; a selector
picks one; ground truth is whether the reference exists. We ran this with
Qwen2.5-1.5B over \nCitTopics{} research topics spanning the sciences, drawing
$k=8$ candidate references per topic, and resolved every candidate against the
OpenAlex catalogue by fuzzy title match (threshold $0.88$), which supplies both
ground truth and one of the two selectors under comparison.

\paragraph{Resolution failures are not fabrications.}
A registry lookup can fail because the paper does not exist or because the
service is temporarily unavailable, and conflating the two would convert a
network condition into a measured hallucination. We retry with exponential
backoff and record any candidate we still cannot resolve as \emph{unresolved}
rather than absent; \nCitUnresolved{} candidates were excluded on this basis,
and a topic contributes to the analysis only if all eight of its candidates
resolved.

\subsection{Results}

The generator fabricates at scale. Of \nCitTopics$\times 8$ candidates,
\citUnparsed{} were not parseable as references at all; of the
\citParseable{} that were, \citHallucRate{} could not be matched to any
indexed work. A single candidate is therefore real with probability
$\citPone{}$, and at least one of eight resolves for only $\citCoverage{}$ of
topics.

\paragraph{The rate depends on the match threshold, the conclusion does not.}
Title matching is a similarity judgement, so we report the whole curve rather
than one cutoff. At our threshold of $0.88$ the fabrication rate is
\citHallucRate{}; relaxing it to $0.80$ gives \citHallucMid{} and to $0.70$
gives \citHallucLoose{}. The median match ratio across all candidates is
\citMedianRatio{}, far below any defensible cutoff. We keep $0.88$ because
inspection shows looser thresholds admit genuinely different papers---a
fabricated ``Graph Neural Networks for Molecular Property Prediction: A
Systematic Review'' matches a real but distinct paper on regularised graph
networks at $0.71$---so the looser numbers understate fabrication rather than
correcting it. What survives every threshold is the qualitative claim: most of
what this generator proposes does not exist.

\paragraph{Neither verifier faces the same problem.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[The model as its own critic.] Asked whether each reference is real, the
  model is not credulous but indiscriminately sceptical: it answers ``real''
  for only \citSaysReal{} of candidates, rejecting \citTNR{} of fabrications
  while also rejecting three quarters of the genuine ones
  ($\mathrm{TPR}=\citTPR{}$). Its balanced accuracy is barely above chance, and
  its pairwise gap is $\gap = \citG{}$---\emph{below} chance, meaning that
  shown one real and one fabricated reference it prefers the fabrication more
  often than not. Its realised selection efficiency is $\eff = \citEtaLLM{}$
  at a cost of \citLLMTokens{} tokens.
\item[The external registry.] The same selection made by a registry lookup
  attains $\eff = \citEtaAPI{}$ at zero model tokens, raising accuracy from
  $\citPone{}$ to $\citAccAPI{}$.
\end{description}

Given only the measured $\gap$, the latent-score model of
Proposition~\ref{prop:latent} predicts $\hat{\eff} = \citPredEta{}$---correctly
anticipating that this critic is worse than useless, without the selection
being run.

\paragraph{What this case study cannot support.}
Coverage here is very low: only six of \nCitTopics{} topics contain any real
reference among their eight candidates, so the comparison between selectors
rests on a handful of items and the efficiencies should be read as directional
rather than precise. The fabrication rate and the gap $\gap$ rest on far more
data (\citParseable{} candidates and every discriminating pair available) and
we report those with more confidence. We also note that a 1.5B model is a
worst case for this task; we would expect a frontier model to fabricate far
less, and we make no claim about where its $\gap$ would sit.

\subsection{What the case study shows}

Two agents that look alike in an architecture diagram---one checks citations,
one polishes prose---are not alike at all under this framework. The first has an
external oracle available for the price of an HTTP request and converts nearly
all of its coverage headroom. The second has no oracle, and a model asked to
supply one performs near chance. The generalisable lesson is that the question
worth asking about a proposed agent is not how capable it is but whether
anything cheap can tell when it is wrong. Where an external check exists, use
it: in our measurements it dominated the model-critic alternative on both
accuracy and cost, which is not a trade-off but a strict improvement.
